Inside the Book

Orcas are not really whales. They are the largest dolphins on Earth, found in every ocean from the polar ice to the tropics, and they live in tight family groups led by their oldest females.

What sets them apart is culture. Pods sharing the same stretch of sea can speak different dialects and eat completely different food, with one chasing herring while another hunts great white sharks. This book looks at how those habits get passed down through the generations, how orcas manage to bring down blue whales, why they never seem to get lost, and why a whole pod mourns when a mother loses her calf.

FACT #4

A Tale of Two Livers

In the waters off South Africa, orcas have developed a highly specific, and slightly gruesome, taste for Great White shark livers. These sharks have massive livers that are incredibly rich in fats and nutrients, providing a high-calorie feast for a hungry orca. The orcas are so precise that they use their snouts to flip the sharks upside down, inducing a state of "tonic immobility" or temporary paralysis. Once the shark is helpless, the orcas make a surgical incision near the pectoral fins and squeeze the liver out like a tube of toothpaste. It’s a terrifying display of biological knowledge and predatory efficiency.

FACT #53

The Mystery of the "Old Tom"

In the early 20th century, a group of wild orcas led by a male known as "Old Tom" developed a partnership with human whalers in Twofold Bay, Australia. The orcas would alert the whalers when a humpback whale was nearby by breaching and tail-slapping. They would then help "herd" the whale toward the boats. In exchange, the whalers would let the orcas eat the humpback's lips and tongue before hauling the rest of the carcass away. This "Law of the Tongue" lasted for nearly 40 years and is one of the most famous examples of cross-species cooperation ever recorded.

FACT #81

The "Whale" that Mimics Others

Orcas are world-class mimics. In captivity, they have been documented mimicking the sounds of bottlenose dolphins, sea lions, and even the "creaking" sound of a rusty gate. There is even a famous recording of an orca named Wikie who was taught to "say" human words like "hello" and "bye-bye" through her blowhole. While they don't understand the words, their ability to replicate complex sounds shows just how flexible their brains are. In the wild, this skill might be used to trick prey or to learn the "language" of a neighboring pod.

FACT #11

The Arctic Ice-Wash Maneuver

Orcas in the Antarctic have figured out how to use physics to knock seals off floating ice floes. When they spot a seal resting on a piece of ice, a group of orcas will swim side-by-side toward it at high speed. Just before they hit the ice, they dive underneath it, creating a synchronized "wave wash" that sweeps over the floe. The force of the water pushes the seal right into the mouths of the waiting pod members on the other side. This level of coordinated teamwork is almost unheard of in the animal kingdom, requiring perfect timing and communication among the hunters.

FACT #41

The Most Toxic Whales

Because orcas are at the very top of the food chain, they suffer from a process called "biomagnification." When a small fish eats a tiny bit of pollution, and a bigger fish eats 100 small fish, the pollution builds up. By the time an orca eats a seal that has eaten those big fish, the orca is consuming a massive dose of toxins like PCBs and flame retardants. In some areas, orcas are so polluted that their bodies are legally classified as "toxic waste" when they wash up on shore. This is one of the biggest threats to their survival, as it can cause infertility and immune system failure.

Frequently Asked Questions About Orcas

What is an orca?

An orca, also known as a killer whale, is the largest member of the oceanic dolphin family. They are apex predators found in all oceans worldwide.

Why are orcas called killer whales?

They are called killer whales due to their powerful predatory skills and ability to hunt large marine animals, including other whales, seals, and sharks.

How intelligent are orcas?

Orcas are highly intelligent, possessing complex social structures, advanced hunting techniques, and problem-solving abilities. They also exhibit cultural learning and communication.

Can orcas be found in all oceans?

Yes, orcas are found in all oceans, from the Arctic and Antarctic regions to tropical waters. They are the most widely distributed marine mammal.

What do orcas eat?

Orcas have diverse diets depending on their ecotype. They eat fish, seals, sea lions, dolphins, porpoises, and even other whales.

Where do orcas live?

Orcas live in all oceans worldwide. Different populations tend to inhabit specific regions, often following prey migrations or staying in coastal waters.

How long do orcas live?

Female orcas can live up to 80-90 years, while males typically live 50-60 years in the wild. Their lifespan is comparable to humans.

Can orcas be domesticated?

No, orcas cannot be domesticated. They are wild animals with complex social needs and vast territories that cannot be replicated in captivity.

Why are orcas important to marine ecosystems?

Orcas are apex predators, meaning they help maintain the balance of marine ecosystems by regulating populations of their prey species. This prevents overgrazing or overpopulation.

What is an orca's social structure like?

Orcas live in highly complex, stable matriarchal family groups called pods. These pods can consist of multiple generations and communicate through unique vocalizations.
