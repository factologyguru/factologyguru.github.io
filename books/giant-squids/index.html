Half a mile down, where sunlight never reaches, lives an animal that sailors told stories about for centuries and scientists could not photograph alive until 2004. The giant squid has eyes the size of dinner plates, blue blood, and a body built for water pressure that would crush a person.

For most of history, people pieced its life together from beaks found in sperm whale stomachs and the odd carcass washed up on a beach. This book follows that detective story, from the old kraken legends to the first video of a living giant in 2012, and shows how an animal this big managed to stay hidden for so long.

FACT #2

A Donut-Shaped Brain Architecture

In one of nature’s strangest design choices, the giant squid has a brain shaped like a literal donut. This circular organ wraps entirely around the animal's esophagus, which is the tube leading from the mouth to the stomach. This means that if the squid swallows a piece of food that is too large or too sharp, it could technically cause physical trauma to its own brain. To prevent self-inflicted brain damage, the squid uses its powerful, parrot-like beak to shred its prey into tiny, manageable ribbons before swallowing.

FACT #10

Ammonia for Natural Buoyancy

Unlike most fish that use a gas-filled swim bladder to stay afloat, the giant squid uses a much smellier solution. Their muscle tissue contains high concentrations of ammonium chloride, which is lighter than seawater. This provides them with "neutral buoyancy," allowing them to hover in the water column without wasting energy swimming to stay up. This is also why giant squid meat is completely inedible to humans; it tastes like concentrated floor cleaner and would make you incredibly sick if you tried to serve it as calamari.

FACT #6

Serrated Teeth on Every Sucker

Unlike the soft, sticky suction cups on a human-friendly octopus, every single sucker on a giant squid is ringed with sharp, serrated teeth made of chitin. Chitin is the same tough material that makes up the shells of crabs and insects. These circular "teeth" act like tiny saws, digging into the flesh of prey to ensure that once the squid grabs something, it never lets go. If you were to touch one, it wouldn't just feel like a vacuum; it would feel like hundreds of tiny cookie cutters pressing into your skin.

FACT #9

Copper-Infused Scars on Whales

We know a lot about giant squid battles because they leave permanent receipts on their enemies. Sperm whales are frequently seen with circular scars on their faces and bodies, left behind by the toothed suckers of a giant squid fighting for its life. These scars often grow as the whale grows, resulting in massive white rings that tell a story of a deep-sea wrestling match. It is a grim reminder that even though the whale is the predator, the giant squid is a formidable opponent that goes down swinging with every weapon it has.

FACT #47

The "Medusa" Camera Discovery

The breakthrough in filming the giant squid came from a device called the "Medusa." Created by Dr. Edith Widder, this camera used "stealth" lighting. Most deep-sea submersibles use bright white lights, which are like a deafening scream to animals that live in total darkness. The Medusa used far-red LEDs, which are invisible to the giant squid but visible to the camera. This allowed scientists to spy on the squid without it ever knowing they were there, finally revealing its natural hunting behavior.

Frequently Asked Questions About Giant Squids

What is a giant squid?

A giant squid (Architeuthis dux) is a deep-sea cephalopod, one of the largest invertebrates on Earth. It has massive eyes and long tentacles, inhabiting the cold, deep oceans worldwide.

How large can giant squids grow?

Giant squids can grow to impressive sizes. Females are typically larger, reaching up to 13 meters (43 feet) in total length, including their two longest feeding tentacles. Males are slightly smaller.

Where do giant squids live?

Giant squids live in all the world's oceans, typically in deep waters ranging from 300 to 1,000 meters (1,000 to 3,300 feet). They prefer cold, nutrient-rich environments.

Why are giant squids so rarely seen?

Giant squids are rarely seen because they inhabit extreme deep-sea environments. Their habitat is largely inaccessible to humans, and they are solitary creatures, making encounters very uncommon.

Can giant squids see in the dark?

Yes, giant squids can see in the dark. They possess the largest eyes of any animal, up to 27 cm (11 inches) in diameter, which are highly adapted for detecting faint light in the deep ocean.

What do giant squids eat?

Giant squids eat deep-sea fish, other squid species, and possibly even smaller giant squids. They use their powerful tentacles and beak to capture and consume prey in the dark depths.

How do giant squids reproduce?

Giant squids reproduce sexually, but the exact details are not fully understood due to their deep-sea habitat. Males use a long hectocotylus arm to transfer spermatophores to the female.

Why do giant squids have such large eyes?

Giant squids have such large eyes to detect faint bioluminescent light in the deep ocean. This helps them spot both prey and predators, like sperm whales, in their dark environment.

Can giant squids be dangerous to humans?

No, giant squids are not considered dangerous to humans. They live in deep waters and there are no documented cases of attacks on humans. Encounters are extremely rare.

What is the main predator of the giant squid?

The main predator of the giant squid is the sperm whale. Evidence from whale stomach contents and scars on whales indicates frequent battles between these two deep-sea giants.
