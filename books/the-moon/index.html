FACT #1

The Massive Celestial Fender Bender

Most scientists agree that the Moon was born from a catastrophic collision about 4.5 billion years ago. A Mars-sized object named Theia slammed into the young Earth with such force that it basically liquified both worlds. A massive cloud of vaporized rock and debris was blasted into orbit, eventually clumping together to form the sphere we see today. This explains why the Moon and Earth share nearly identical chemical signatures; they are essentially made of the same stuff. It was a violent beginning, but without that planetary car crash, Earth would be a very different, much more unstable place. The Moon is effectively a piece of our own planet that got knocked loose and decided to stay close.

FACT #2

The First Map of Shadows

In 1609, Thomas Harriot beat Galileo to the punch by four months, becoming the first person to draw a map of the Moon using a telescope. While Galileo gets all the fame for his detailed sketches, Harriot was the true pioneer of lunar cartography. He looked at the rugged surface and realized it wasn't a perfect, smooth heavenly orb as the Greeks had believed. Instead, he saw a world of jagged mountains and deep pits. These early drawings shifted our entire worldview, proving that other worlds were physical places with geography similar to our own. It was the moment the Moon stopped being a god and started being a destination.

FACT #4

The Ultimate High-Tech Laser Reflector

During the Apollo missions, astronauts left behind several small arrays of mirrors called Retroreflectors. Scientists on Earth still use these today by firing powerful laser beams at the Moon and timing how long it takes for the light to bounce back. This allows us to measure the distance between Earth and the Moon with a precision of just a few millimeters. It is how we know for a fact that the Moon is drifting away from us. These simple mirror panels are some of the most enduring pieces of technology ever placed on another world, operating without any electricity or maintenance for over fifty years.

FACT #17

Earthrise: The Photo That Changed Us

On Christmas Eve in 1968, the crew of Apollo 8 became the first humans to see the Earth rising over the lunar horizon. Astronaut Bill Anders snapped a color photo of the vibrant blue marble hanging in the black void above the barren gray Moon. This image, known as "Earthrise," is credited with sparking the modern environmental movement. Seeing our entire planet as a small, fragile, and lonely island in the dark changed the way millions of people thought about our world. It proved that the most important thing we discovered during the race to the Moon was actually the Earth itself.

FACT #32

Moon Trees are Growing on Earth

During the Apollo 14 mission in 1971, astronaut Stuart Roosa took about 500 seeds into lunar orbit. These included redwood, loblolly pine, sycamore, Douglas fir, and sweetgum seeds. After returning to Earth, the seeds were germinated, and the resulting "Moon Trees" were planted all across the United States and even in a few international locations. Surprisingly, the trees grew perfectly normally, showing that a trip to the Moon didn't have any negative effects on the biology of the seeds. Today, you can visit many of these trees in places like state capitals and NASA centers, living monuments to a very strange botanical experiment.

Frequently Asked Questions About The Moon

Why does the Moon appear to change shape?

The Moon appears to change shape because we see different amounts of its sunlit surface as it orbits Earth. This phenomenon is called lunar phases, cycling from new moon to full moon.

How big is the Moon compared to Earth?

The Moon is about one-quarter the diameter of Earth. Specifically, its diameter is approximately 3,474 kilometers, making it the fifth-largest moon in our solar system.

What is the Moon made of?

The Moon is primarily made of silicate rocks, similar to Earth's mantle, but with a smaller iron core. Its surface is covered in regolith, a layer of dusty, broken rock.

Can humans live on the Moon?

Humans cannot currently live on the Moon without significant life support systems. It lacks a breathable atmosphere, liquid water, and is exposed to extreme temperatures and radiation.

Where did the Moon come from?

The most accepted theory is the giant-impact hypothesis, suggesting the Moon formed from debris ejected into orbit after a Mars-sized body collided with early Earth.

How long does a day last on the Moon?

A day on the Moon, from one sunrise to the next, lasts approximately 29.5 Earth days. This is because its rotation period matches its orbital period around Earth.

Why does the Moon have so many craters?

The Moon has many craters because it lacks a significant atmosphere to burn up incoming meteoroids and geological activity (like plate tectonics or erosion) to erase ancient impact scars.

What causes tides on Earth?

Tides on Earth are primarily caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon, and to a lesser extent, the Sun. The Moon's gravity creates bulges of water on opposite sides of Earth.

Can we see the "dark side" of the Moon from Earth?

No, we cannot see the "dark side" (more accurately, the far side) of the Moon from Earth. The Moon is tidally locked, meaning the same side always faces Earth.

How many astronauts have walked on the Moon?

Twelve astronauts have walked on the Moon. All were American men from the Apollo missions, with the first being Neil Armstrong in 1969 and the last Eugene Cernan in 1972.
