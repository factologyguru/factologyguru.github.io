Inside the Book

The International Space Station circles Earth every 90 minutes at about 17,500 miles per hour, so the crew sees 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every day. People have lived on board without a break since November 2000, which means someone has been off the planet for more than 25 years straight.

This book covers the station from every angle: how it was bolted together in orbit from parts built in different countries, how the crew recycles its water, sleeps strapped to a wall and drinks from sealed pouches, and what months in microgravity do to bones, eyes and hearts. It also looks at the science done on board and at the plan to retire the station around 2030 by steering it into the remote Pacific Ocean.

5 Facts From the Book

FACT #1

The Fastest House in the Neighborhood

The ISS orbits Earth at a blistering speed of 17,500 miles per hour, which is roughly twenty-five times the speed of sound. At this pace, the astronauts on board aren't just "floating"; they are essentially in a state of constant freefall, moving sideways so fast that they miss the Earth every time they fall toward it. This creates the environment we call microgravity, where weight disappears but mass remains. Because the station circles the globe every 90 minutes, the crew witnesses 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every single day. Imagine trying to set an alarm clock when your "day" only lasts an hour and a half and the sun is constantly popping in and out of the window.

FACT #4

The Water Cycle of Your Dreams

On the ISS, water is far too heavy to launch in bulk, so the station uses a high-tech Recovery System that recycles about 93% of all fluids. This includes everything from breath moisture and sweat to the astronauts' urine, which is filtered and purified back into drinking water. The crew often jokes that today’s coffee is tomorrow’s coffee, as the filtration system is so effective the water is actually cleaner than most municipal tap water on Earth. This closed-loop technology is more than just a space gimmick; it is currently being used in disaster zones and developing nations to provide clean drinking water where infrastructure is non-existent.

FACT #5

The Mystery of the Missing Muscle

When humans live in microgravity, their bodies decide they no longer need the heavy bones and muscles required to fight Earth's gravity. Without constant resistance, an astronaut can lose up to 1% of their bone density every single month, which is a rate of bone loss similar to severe osteoporosis. To combat this "melting" of the human frame, residents must exercise for at least two hours every day using specialized bungee-cord treadmills and vacuum-based weightlifting machines. Scientists are still trying to figure out exactly how to stop this process entirely, as it remains one of the biggest hurdles for a three-year round trip to Mars.

FACT #2

A Global LEGO Project in Orbit

Construction of the ISS began in 1998 with a Russian module named Zarya, which provided power and propulsion for the infant station. Over the next thirteen years, NASA, Roscosmos, the ESA, JAXA, and the CSA launched over 40 separate missions to bolt on new rooms, solar arrays, and docking ports. Many of these modules were built in different countries and had never touched each other until they met 250 miles above the Earth. It is the ultimate testament to precision engineering that these massive components, built using the metric system in Europe and the imperial system in the US, fit together with millimeter-perfect accuracy in the vacuum of space.

FACT #3

The Most Expensive Kitchen Sink

With a price tag exceeding 150 billion dollars, the ISS is officially the most expensive object ever built by the human race. To put that in perspective, you could buy roughly 300 Boeing 747s or build twenty Great Pyramids of Giza for the same amount of money. The high cost comes from the sheer difficulty of launching heavy materials into orbit, which historically cost about 10,000 dollars per pound. Every nut, bolt, and laptop on board had to be vetted for safety and durability in a high-radiation environment. While the price is staggering, the scientific breakthroughs in medicine and materials science provide a return on investment that impacts almost everyone on the planet.

Frequently Asked Questions About The International Space Station

What is the International Space Station?

The International Space Station (ISS) is a habitable artificial satellite, a multi-nation collaborative project, and a research laboratory in low Earth orbit. It serves as a microgravity and space environment research platform.

How does the ISS stay in orbit?

The ISS stays in orbit by continuously falling around the Earth. Its high orbital velocity creates a centripetal force that balances Earth's gravity, keeping it from falling back to the surface.

Why was the ISS built?

The ISS was built to serve as a microgravity and space environment research laboratory, allowing scientists to conduct experiments in biology, physics, astronomy, and meteorology. It also tests spacecraft systems for future long-duration missions.

What agencies operate the International Space Station?

NASA (USA), Roscosmos (Russia), ESA (Europe), JAXA (Japan), and CSA (Canada) jointly operate the ISS. These five space agencies manage its modules, missions, and astronaut crews.

How fast does the ISS travel?

The ISS travels at approximately 28,000 kilometers per hour (17,500 mph). This incredible speed allows it to orbit Earth about 16 times per day, completing one full orbit every 90 minutes.

Where is the International Space Station located?

The ISS is located in low Earth orbit (LEO), typically at an altitude of about 400 kilometers (250 miles) above the Earth's surface. Its orbital path covers most of the populated landmasses.

Can you see the ISS from Earth?

Yes, you can often see the ISS from Earth. It is the third brightest object in the night sky, after the Moon and Venus, appearing as a bright, fast-moving point of light.

What kind of research is done on the ISS?

Research on the ISS covers diverse fields including human physiology, biology, materials science, fluid physics, Earth observation, and fundamental physics. It helps prepare for future long-duration space exploration.

How big is the International Space Station?

The ISS is roughly the size of a football field (109 meters/357 feet long). Its habitable volume is comparable to a five-bedroom house, providing ample space for its crew and equipment.

How long has the ISS been continuously inhabited?

The ISS has been continuously inhabited for over 23 years, since November 2, 2000. It holds the record for the longest continuous human presence in space.
