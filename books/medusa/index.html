Inside the Book

Most people know the version where a beautiful priestess is cursed with snakes for hair. That story comes from the Roman poet Ovid, writing around 8 CE. The Greeks who came centuries before him, starting with Hesiod, described Medusa as a monster from birth, one of three Gorgon sisters and the only one who could die.

This book follows both versions side by side, through myth, art, coins and medicine, all the way to the jellyfish that still carry her name. It also shows why her face ended up on shields, doorways and money across the ancient world, where people believed it could stop danger cold before it ever reached them.

5 Facts From the Book

FACT #3

Ovid's Beauty, Not History's

The idea that Medusa was once a legendary beauty comes almost entirely from one source: Ovid's Metamorphoses, written around 8 CE. Ovid describes her hair as her most magnificent feature, the thing that drew every eye in Greece toward her. Earlier Greek writers never bother with this detail because in their tradition she was never human to begin with. Hesiod's Gorgons were born monstrous, full stop. Ovid was not reporting old folklore, he was writing Roman literary tragedy, reshaping a monster into a victim to make a point about power and cruelty. Treat it as Ovid's deliberate invention, not the ancient Greek consensus, and the whole myth gets more interesting, not less.

FACT #2

The Sister Who Could Actually Die

Medusa was the youngest of three Gorgon sisters, and also the only one built to lose. Her older sisters, Stheno and Euryale, were immortal, untouchable by blade or by time. Medusa alone was mortal, vulnerable to a sword the same way any human was. That single biological quirk decided the entire myth. Perseus never stood a chance against Stheno or Euryale; killing an immortal being simply is not how Greek mythology works. Medusa's mortality was not an insult bolted onto her story. It was the mechanism that made her killable, the one design flaw in an otherwise indestructible family that a desperate king could actually exploit.

FACT #30

Why a Fashion Empire Wears Her Face

In 1978, Gianni Versace chose Medusa as his brand's logo after childhood memories of Greek ruins near his hometown of Reggio Calabria. He later explained the choice directly: he wanted a symbol of fatal attraction, someone whose gaze could stop people in their tracks the moment they looked at it. Whether he meant her pre- or post-transformation face is not something Versace documented precisely, but the symbolic logic is on record in his own words. Today her face sits on handbags and perfume bottles worldwide, still doing the exact job the ancient Greeks assigned her three thousand years earlier.

FACT #40

A Medical Term Doctors Still Use

In advanced cirrhosis of the liver, blocked blood flow through the portal vein forces circulation through smaller surrounding veins, causing the ones around a patient's navel to swell into a visible, radiating web. Physicians officially call this clinical sign caput medusae, Latin for "head of Medusa," because the pattern of engorged veins genuinely resembles snakes radiating from a central point. It is one of medicine's more poetic diagnostic names, a three-thousand-year-old myth still doing useful work in a modern emergency room, describing a symptom doctors need to recognize on sight.

FACT #35

Upside Down Beneath Istanbul

Deep in Istanbul's sixth-century Basilica Cistern, two massive marble Gorgon heads support column bases, one turned completely upside down, the other tilted sideways. Both were reused architectural fragments taken from older buildings by the Byzantine builders who constructed the reservoir. Why they were positioned this way remains genuinely debated among archaeologists and art historians; some read it as symbolic dominance over pagan imagery, others as simple practical reuse, stone cut to whatever size the columns needed. No inscription settles it. The cistern keeps the mystery exactly as intact as the two staring, disoriented faces themselves.

Frequently Asked Questions About Medusa

What is Medusa best known for?

Medusa is best known for her head of venomous snakes instead of hair and her ability to turn onlookers to stone with her gaze.

How was Medusa transformed?

Medusa was transformed by the goddess Athena as punishment, often cited for defiling Athena's temple with Poseidon.

What hero eventually defeated Medusa?

Perseus, a demigod hero, eventually defeated Medusa by beheading her while looking at her reflection in his shield.

Where did Medusa live?

Medusa lived on a remote island, often specified as Sarpedon, at the ends of the earth, away from mortals.

Why was Medusa considered a monster?

Medusa was considered a monster due to her terrifying appearance, her petrifying gaze, and her lineage as one of the Gorgons.

Can Medusa's story be interpreted differently?

Yes, some modern interpretations view Medusa as a victim or a powerful female figure, rather than solely a monster.

What happened after Medusa's death?

From Medusa's severed neck sprang Pegasus, the winged horse, and Chrysaor, the golden giant, fathered by Poseidon.

How did Perseus use Medusa's head?

Perseus used Medusa's head as a weapon to turn his enemies to stone, including Atlas and Polydectes, before giving it to Athena.

Where did Athena place Medusa's head?

Athena placed Medusa's head on her aegis, a shield or breastplate, as a powerful protective and intimidating emblem.

What were Medusa's sisters called?

Medusa's sisters were Stheno and Euryale. All three were known as the Gorgons, but Medusa was the only mortal one.
