Inside the Book

William Kidd spent most of his life as a respectable New York merchant. He married a wealthy widow, lived in a house on Wall Street and lent his ship's tackle to help build Trinity Church. In 1696, a group of powerful English lords and a colonial governor backed him with a royal commission to hunt pirates and French ships in the Indian Ocean. Five years later, he was hanged as a pirate at Execution Dock in London.

This book follows how that happened: two years with almost no prizes, a sick and mutinous crew, the capture of the Quedagh Merchant, and the backers who dropped him once the politics turned. It also separates the real Kidd from the legend, from the goods he actually left on Gardiner's Island to the cursed hoard people have hunted for three centuries.

5 Facts From the Book

FACT #9

The Insolent Send-off Down the Thames

Kidd's voyage nearly ended before it began. Sailing down the Thames in April 1696, he neglected to dip his colors to a passing Royal Navy vessel, a serious breach of maritime custom. The navy answered with a cannonball fired across his bow. Kidd's crew responded by turning around and slapping their own backsides at the warship. Furious, a navy press gang boarded the Adventure Galley shortly after and made off with twenty of Kidd's best men. He rowed ashore waving his commission in protest, and the navy eventually returned twenty replacements, but not his twenty. He got back a boatload of troublemakers instead, and the voyage had not even cleared English waters yet.

FACT #49

Stolen by His Own First Mate

Kidd's rivalry with the pirate Robert Culliford started years before either man ever set foot near Madagascar. In 1690, while Kidd's ship lay anchored at Antigua, Culliford, then serving as Kidd's own first mate, stole the vessel out from under him and marooned Kidd on the island. It was a personal betrayal Kidd never forgot. When the two men crossed paths again at St. Mary's Island in 1698, that decade-old theft was still very much unfinished business, and it shaped exactly how Kidd reacted when he spotted Culliford's ship anchored in the same harbor.

FACT #30

The House on Wall Street

Sarah's fortune bought the Kidds a tall house on Wall Street, furnished with imported Turkish carpets and a well-stocked wine cellar, a home suited to one of New York's more prominent couples. Kidd took real pride in his standing in the city, going as far as lending his ship's own rope and tackle to help raise the stones for the construction of Trinity Church, where he later held his own pew. This was not a man living rough between voyages. It was a respected citizen with a genuine stake in his community, which makes the contrast with his eventual fate at Execution Dock all the more striking.

FACT #90

The Wreck Rediscovered

For three centuries, the fate of the Quedagh Merchant, the ship at the center of Kidd's entire downfall, remained a mystery. In 2007, researchers from Indiana University discovered its wreck accidentally in shallow water off Catalina Island in the Dominican Republic, still bearing cannons encrusted in coral after Kidd's associates burned and scuttled it there in 1699 to destroy evidence. The find turned into a genuine archaeological event, confirming details of the ship's construction and cargo capacity that historians had previously only been able to estimate from trial records and secondhand accounts of Kidd's most consequential capture.

FACT #75

Too Drunk to Notice

Eyewitnesses at Kidd's execution reported something strange about the man facing the gallows that day. He appeared to be drunk, and when the first rope snapped and he dropped to the ground still alive, he reportedly did not seem to fully grasp what had just happened as the crowd erupted into cheers around him. Whether he was given drink deliberately to steady his nerves or simply drank out of dread beforehand is not entirely clear from the record. Either way, one of the most infamous moments in piracy's history involved a condemned man who, by several accounts, barely registered his own brush with survival.

Frequently Asked Questions About Captain Kidd

What was Captain Kidd primarily known for?

Captain William Kidd was primarily known as a Scottish privateer who later gained infamy as a pirate. His controversial career spanned the late 17th century, marked by a shift from hunting pirates to being accused of piracy himself.

Where was Captain Kidd born?

Captain William Kidd was likely born in Dundee, Scotland, around 1645. He later moved to New York, where he established himself as a respected merchant and ship owner before embarking on his fateful privateering voyage.

How did Captain Kidd transition from privateer to pirate?

Captain Kidd transitioned when his crew, facing starvation and lack of plunder, pressured him to attack merchant ships, including the Quedagh Merchant. This act, along with others, led to his condemnation as a pirate.

What was the name of Captain Kidd's most famous ship?

Captain Kidd's most famous ship was the Adventure Galley. It was a 287-ton, 34-gun vessel, specifically designed for his privateering mission to hunt pirates and French ships in the Indian Ocean.

What is known about Captain Kidd's buried treasure?

Captain Kidd did bury some treasure, notably on Gardiner's Island, New York, before his arrest. However, the vast, fabled treasure chests often associated with him are largely a myth, exaggerated by folklore and fiction.

How was Captain Kidd eventually captured?

Captain Kidd was eventually captured in Boston in 1699, after being lured there by Governor Bellomont with false promises of clemency. He was then sent to England to stand trial for piracy and murder.

What was the date of Captain Kidd's execution?

Captain Kidd was executed on May 23, 1701. He was hanged at Execution Dock in Wapping, London, for the murder of his gunner, William Moore, and for acts of piracy.

Why is Captain Kidd still a famous figure today?

Captain Kidd remains famous today due to the enduring legends of his buried treasure and his controversial transformation from a commissioned privateer to a condemned pirate. His story has inspired countless tales and treasure hunts.

What was the Quedagh Merchant incident?

The Quedagh Merchant incident involved Captain Kidd capturing the Armenian ship, the Quedagh Merchant, in 1698. This act, rich with silks and spices, was a definitive turning point, solidifying his reputation as a pirate to British authorities.

Can you explain Captain Kidd's original privateering commission?

Captain Kidd received a privateering commission in 1696 from King William III to suppress piracy and attack French ships in the Indian Ocean. This venture was backed by powerful English noblemen, hoping for significant returns.
