Frequently Asked Questions About The Big Bang

What is the Big Bang theory?

It is the leading cosmological model explaining the universe's earliest known periods. It describes how the universe expanded from an extremely hot, dense state.

How long ago did the Big Bang occur?

It occurred approximately 13.8 billion years ago. This age is determined by measuring the expansion rate of the universe and extrapolating backward.

What happened immediately after the Big Bang?

Immediately after the Big Bang, the universe underwent a rapid expansion called cosmic inflation. It cooled as it expanded, allowing fundamental particles to form.

Why is it called the "Big Bang"?

The name "Big Bang" was coined derisively by astronomer Fred Hoyle in 1949, who favored a "steady-state" model. The name stuck, despite its initial intent.

Can we "see" the Big Bang?

No, we cannot directly "see" the Big Bang itself. However, we can observe its afterglow, known as the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation.

What is the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)?

The CMB is the oldest light in the universe, a faint glow of radiation filling all space. It is leftover heat from the very early universe, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang.

How does the Big Bang theory explain the expansion of the universe?

The Big Bang theory posits that space itself is expanding, carrying galaxies along with it. This expansion causes distant galaxies to appear to move away from us.

Where did the Big Bang happen?

The Big Bang didn't happen at a specific point in space. It was an expansion of space everywhere. Every point in the universe was once contained within that initial singularity.

What evidence supports the Big Bang theory?

Key evidence includes the expansion of the universe (Hubble's Law), the Cosmic Microwave Background radiation, and the observed abundance of light elements (hydrogen, helium, lithium).

Can the Big Bang explain what existed before it?

No, the Big Bang theory describes the evolution of the universe from a specific initial state, not what might have preceded it. Our current physics breaks down at that singularity.
