Inside the Book

The capybara is the largest rodent alive today, roughly the weight of a grown man, and somehow the calmest animal on the riverbank. Birds ride on its back, monkeys nap against it, and even caimans tend to leave it alone.

The book opens with that strange reputation and then looks at what sits behind it: teeth that sharpen themselves, a gut that gets two tries at every meal, eyes and nostrils set high like a snorkel, and a whole vocabulary of barks, whistles and purrs. From prehistoric relatives that topped 400 pounds to the hot spring baths that made them famous in Japan, these 100 facts follow the capybara from the wetlands of South America all the way to the internet.

FACT #7

Vatican Classified Them as Fish

The Catholic Church approved a clergy petition, submitted over the 16th to 18th centuries, to classify the capybara as a "fish" so that settlers could eat its meat during Lent. Because they spend so much time in the water and have webbed feet, the clergy argued they qualified as seafood rather than red meat. This strange historical loophole led to the capybara becoming a traditional delicacy in certain regions, particularly in Venezuela, where capybara stew is still a common sight during religious holidays. It remains one of the oddest examples of human cultural classification overriding biological reality.

FACT #5

A Digestive System That Recycles

Capybaras practice "autocoprophagy," which is a fancy way of saying they eat their own morning droppings. These specific droppings, known as cecotropes, are rich in beneficial bacteria and undigested protein, helping them squeeze every nutrient out of tough, fibrous grasses. Because grass is notoriously difficult to digest, passing it through the system twice ensures that the capybara gets maximum caloric value. Without this secondary digestion process, they would likely starve to death even in a field of grass, as their primary digestion can't break down the cellulose efficiently on the first pass.

FACT #44

The "Moving Island" Phenomenon

During floods, capybaras have been known to climb onto floating mats of vegetation to survive. These "floating islands" can sometimes drift for miles, carrying the rodents to new territories. This is likely how they managed to spread across such a vast portion of South America over thousands of years. Being on a floating island also provides a temporary reprieve from land predators, though they have to be careful of large snakes that might also be seeking refuge on the same mat. It’s a literal life-raft in the middle of a flooded world.

FACT #16

Giant Ancestors from the Past

Millions of years ago, prehistoric relatives of the capybara called Neochoerus roamed North and South America. These ancient "mega-rodents" were significantly larger than today's version, weighing over 400 pounds. They lived alongside other giants like the glyptodon (a car-sized armadillo) and giant ground sloths. While most of these mega-mammals went extinct at the end of the last Ice Age, the capybara's lineage survived by "downsizing" to a more manageable size that required less food and could hide more effectively in modern wetlands.

FACT #84

The "Yuzu" Bath Tradition Origin

The Japanese tradition of putting capybaras in hot baths started by accident in 1982. A zookeeper at Izu Shaboten Zoo noticed capybaras huddling in a puddle of hot water used for cleaning and realized they loved it. He then decided to build them a proper hot spring, and a cultural icon was born. Adding yuzu (a citrus fruit) was a later addition, based on a Japanese human tradition of taking citrus baths for health during the winter solstice. The capybaras loved the scent and the warmth, and the image of a capybara with a lemon on its head became a worldwide internet sensation.

Frequently Asked Questions About Capybara

What is a capybara?

A capybara is the largest living rodent, native to South America. They are semi-aquatic mammals known for their calm demeanor and social nature.

Where do capybaras live?

Capybaras live in South America, primarily in savannas and dense forests near bodies of water like rivers, lakes, swamps, and ponds.

What do capybaras eat?

Capybaras primarily eat grasses and aquatic plants. They are herbivores and will also consume fruits and tree bark when available.

How do capybaras behave socially?

Capybaras are highly social animals, living in groups of 10-20 individuals. They communicate through various vocalizations and scent marking.

Why do capybaras love water?

Capybaras love water because they are semi-aquatic. They use water to cool down, escape predators, and even mate. They can hold their breath for up to five minutes.

How big can capybaras get?

Capybaras can grow up to about 4 feet (1.2 meters) long and weigh over 150 pounds (68 kg). They are stocky with short legs and partially webbed feet.

How long do capybaras live?

Capybaras typically live for 8-10 years in captivity, but only 3-4 years in the wild due to predation. They breed once or twice a year.

Can capybaras swim well?

Yes, capybaras can swim exceptionally well. Their partially webbed feet and ability to hold their breath make them adept in water, often submerging to hide.

Why do other animals sit on capybaras?

Other animals sometimes sit on capybaras due to their calm, docile nature. Birds often perch on them to forage for insects, demonstrating a unique symbiotic relationship.

What are the main threats to capybaras?

The main threats to capybaras are habitat destruction, hunting for their meat and skin, and predation by jaguars, anacondas, and caimans.
