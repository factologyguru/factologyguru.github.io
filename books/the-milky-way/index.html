Frequently Asked Questions About The Milky Way

What is the Milky Way?

The Milky Way is the spiral galaxy that contains our Solar System. It's home to billions of stars, gas, dust, and dark matter, all bound together by gravity.

How big is the Milky Way?

The Milky Way is approximately 100,000 to 120,000 light-years in diameter. It contains an estimated 100 to 400 billion stars, along with vast amounts of gas and dust.

Where is Earth located in the Milky Way?

Earth is located in one of the Milky Way's spiral arms, specifically the Orion Arm (or Local Arm), about two-thirds of the way out from the galactic center.

Why is it called the Milky Way?

It's called the Milky Way because of its appearance from Earth: a hazy band of light across the night sky formed by countless stars that cannot be individually distinguished by the naked eye.

What type of galaxy is the Milky Way?

The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy. It features a prominent bar-shaped structure of stars at its center, with two major spiral arms extending outwards.

How old is the Milky Way?

The Milky Way is estimated to be around 13.6 billion years old, making it nearly as old as the universe itself. Its oldest stars formed shortly after the Big Bang.

Can we see the entire Milky Way from Earth?

No, we cannot see the entire Milky Way from Earth. Our view is obscured by vast clouds of dust and gas within the galaxy, especially towards the galactic center.

What is at the center of the Milky Way?

At the center of the Milky Way is a supermassive black hole called Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*). It has a mass equivalent to about 4 million Suns.

Why is the Milky Way going to collide with Andromeda?

The Milky Way is going to collide with Andromeda because both galaxies are gravitationally attracted to each other and are moving towards one another. This will happen in about 4.5 billion years.

How fast is the Solar System moving through the Milky Way?

The Solar System orbits the center of the Milky Way at an average speed of about 230 kilometers per second (514,000 mph). It takes roughly 230 million years to complete one orbit.
