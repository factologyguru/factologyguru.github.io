5 Facts From the Book

FACT #2

A Discovery Made by Blink

In 1930, a 24-year-old farm boy named Clyde Tombaugh discovered Pluto using nothing but a mechanical device called a blink comparator. He spent months looking at pairs of photographic plates of the sky taken nights apart, clicking back and forth between them to see if any "star" moved. Most stars stayed perfectly still, but a tiny, faint speck jumped positions, revealing a new world. This wasn't a high-tech computer algorithm; it was the result of human patience and the sharpest eyes in Kansas. His discovery expanded the known borders of our solar system by billions of miles in a single afternoon.

FACT #4

The Piano Sized Speed Demon

To reach Pluto, NASA built the New Horizons spacecraft, which was roughly the size and shape of a grand piano. Launched in 2006, it became the fastest man-made object ever to leave Earth's orbit, screaming away at over 36,000 miles per hour. Even at that blistering speed, it took nine and a half years of straight-line travel to reach its target. To save power during the long cruise, the spacecraft spent most of its time in "hibernation," waking up once a year to check its systems and send a "beep" back home. It was a masterpiece of endurance and precision engineering.

FACT #20

Discovery of the Name

The name "Pluto" wasn't chosen by a team of elite scientists, but by an 11-year-old girl named Venetia Burney from Oxford, England. After the discovery in 1930, her grandfather mentioned it over breakfast, and Venetia suggested the name of the Roman god of the underworld who could make himself invisible. Her grandfather passed the suggestion to a friend who was an astronomy professor, who then cabled it to the Lowell Observatory. The staff loved it, partly because the first two letters, "P" and "L," were the initials of Percival Lowell, the man who started the search. Venetia received five pounds as a reward for her world-naming contribution.

FACT #28

Physics of the Nitrogen Convection

The giant heart of Pluto is divided into a pattern of polygons that look like giant cellular structures or "paving stones." These shapes are about 10 to 25 miles across and are caused by convection, the same process that makes soup bubble in a pot. The nitrogen ice is warmed from below, rises to the surface, cools, and sinks back down in a slow-motion churn. Each cell takes about 500,000 years to complete one "bubble." It is a geologic lava lamp on a planetary scale, proving that even "frozen" worlds can be in constant motion.

FACT #48

Extreme Stats of the Day-Night Cycle

A single day on Pluto (one full rotation) lasts about 6.4 Earth days, which is exactly the same amount of time it takes Charon to orbit. This is called "tidal locking," and it means that Pluto and Charon always show the same face to each other. If you lived on the side of Pluto facing Charon, the moon would hang perfectly still in the sky, never rising or setting. If you lived on the other side, you would never see the moon at all. It is a permanent, unchanging celestial relationship that makes Pluto's "nights" feel incredibly long and still.

Frequently Asked Questions About Pluto

What is Pluto?

Pluto is a dwarf planet located in the Kuiper Belt, a region of icy bodies beyond Neptune. It was once considered the ninth planet before its reclassification.

Why was Pluto reclassified as a dwarf planet?

Pluto was reclassified in 2006 because it has not "cleared its orbit" of other debris, a key criterion for full planetary status. Its smaller size was also a factor.

How big is Pluto compared to Earth?

Pluto is much smaller than Earth, with a diameter about two-thirds that of Earth's Moon. It is roughly 1,473 miles (2,370 km) across, making it smaller than several moons.

Can Pluto support life?

No, Pluto is extremely cold and has a very thin atmosphere, making it unsuitable for known forms of life. Its surface temperature averages around -387°F (-232°C).

Where is Pluto located?

Pluto is located in the Kuiper Belt, a vast ring of icy bodies and dwarf planets that lies beyond the orbit of Neptune in our solar system. Its orbit is highly eccentric.

What is Pluto's atmosphere like?

Pluto's atmosphere is very thin, composed mainly of nitrogen, methane, and carbon monoxide. It expands when Pluto is closer to the Sun and collapses as it moves away.

How many moons does Pluto have?

Pluto has five known moons. The largest and most prominent is Charon, which is so large it forms a binary system with Pluto, orbiting a common center of mass.

How was Pluto discovered?

Pluto was discovered in 1930 by Clyde Tombaugh, who systematically compared photographic plates of the night sky, looking for moving objects predicted by planetary perturbations.

Why is Pluto so cold?

Pluto is so cold because it is very far from the Sun, receiving minimal solar radiation. Its average distance from the Sun is about 3.7 billion miles (5.9 billion km).

What is the Kuiper Belt?

The Kuiper Belt is a vast disc-shaped region of icy bodies beyond Neptune's orbit, extending from about 30 to 50 AU from the Sun. It contains many dwarf planets, including Pluto.
