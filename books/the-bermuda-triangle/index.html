Inside the Book

In December 1945, five Navy torpedo bombers known as Flight 19 lost their bearings over calm blue water and were never found. That one flight turned a patch of the Atlantic between Miami, Bermuda and Puerto Rico into the most famous stretch of ocean on the planet.

The odd part is that no official map shows the Triangle at all. This book looks at both sides of the story: the ships and planes that vanished without leaving a trace, the theories that followed, from methane bubbles to magnetic tricks, and the fast-moving Gulf Stream that can scatter a wreck in minutes. It is as much about how the ocean works as it is about why people love a good mystery.

FACT #4

Christopher Columbus Saw Weird Lights

The mystery goes back much further than the 20th century; even Christopher Columbus had a bad time here in 1492. In his ship's logs, he noted that the stars seemed to move out of place and that his compass acted erratically near the Sargasso Sea. Most famously, he and his crew reported seeing a "great flame of fire" crash into the ocean one night. While modern historians think he might have seen a meteor or light from a distant shore fire, it set the tone for the area. Even the man who "discovered" the Americas thought the water there felt a little bit off.

FACT #9

The Ocean Swallowed a Giant

In March 1918, the USS Cyclops, a massive Navy collier ship carrying 300 people and a heavy load of manganese ore, vanished without a trace. It was a 542-foot-long beast of a ship, yet it sent no SOS and left no debris field. At the time, it was the single largest loss of life in US Navy history not involving combat. Some think the ship was overloaded and simply capsized in a sudden storm. Others point to the fact that one of its engines was broken. Regardless of the cause, the ocean swallowed a giant without even burping.

FACT #20

Vincent Gaddis Coined the Name

The term "Bermuda Triangle" didn't exist until 1964. It was coined by writer Vincent Gaddis in a cover story for a pulp magazine called Argosy. Before that, people called it the "Hoodoo Sea" or the "Graveyard of the Atlantic." Gaddis was the one who drew the lines on the map and claimed there was a pattern to the disappearances. He was a master of "creative non-fiction," which is a polite way of saying he knew how to spice up a story to sell magazines. The legend was born from the stroke of a journalist’s pen.

FACT #27

The World Wildlife Fund’s Verdict

In 2013, the World Wildlife Fund (WWF) conducted an exhaustive study of the world's most dangerous waters for shipping. They identified the top ten deadliest spots based on accident data. The Bermuda Triangle didn't even make the list. The most dangerous spots were actually the South China Sea, the Mediterranean, and the North Sea. This is a massive "reality check" for the Triangle legend. It suggests that while the Triangle has a great PR department, it isn't actually the deadliest place to work on a boat.

FACT #94

The Lucky Dog

The Rubicon was found drifting off the coast of Florida in 1944. The only living thing on board was a dog. The lifeboats were gone, and the crew had vanished. This was during World War II, and the ship was near a major combat zone. While "ghost ship" stories are fun, the most likely explanation is that the crew saw something, like a German sub, and panicked, abandoning ship in the lifeboats, which were then lost at sea. The dog stayed behind and survived.

Frequently Asked Questions About The Bermuda Triangle

What is the Bermuda Triangle?

The Bermuda Triangle is a loosely defined region in the western part of the North Atlantic Ocean. It's known for a number of aircraft and ships disappearing mysteriously under unknown circumstances.

Where is the Bermuda Triangle located?

It is located in the western North Atlantic Ocean, with its three "corners" typically defined by Bermuda, Miami (Florida), and San Juan (Puerto Rico).

Why is it called the Bermuda Triangle?

The name "Bermuda Triangle" gained popularity after articles in the 1960s and 70s highlighted a pattern of mysterious disappearances within this specific geographic area.

How many disappearances are attributed to the Bermuda Triangle?

Estimates vary widely, but hundreds of ships and planes, and thousands of lives, have been lost in the Bermuda Triangle region over the past century.

Can scientific explanations account for the disappearances?

Yes, many scientists and experts attribute disappearances to natural phenomena like severe weather, magnetic anomalies, human error, and the area's heavy shipping traffic.

What are some famous incidents associated with it?

Famous incidents include the disappearance of Flight 19 (a squadron of five US Navy torpedo bombers) in 1945 and the USS Cyclops in 1918.

Why do some people believe in supernatural explanations?

Some believe in supernatural explanations due to the seemingly inexplicable nature of some disappearances, the lack of wreckage, and the dramatic storytelling surrounding the incidents.

How deep are the waters in the Bermuda Triangle?

The waters in the Bermuda Triangle are extremely deep, especially within the Puerto Rico Trench, which is the deepest part of the Atlantic Ocean, making recovery difficult.

What role does methane hydrate play in theories?

Methane hydrate release is a theory suggesting that gas eruptions from the seabed could reduce water density, causing ships to sink rapidly, or affect aircraft.

Can the Bermuda Triangle affect navigation equipment?

Yes, some theories suggest that magnetic anomalies within the region could interfere with compasses and navigation equipment, leading to disorientation and accidents.
