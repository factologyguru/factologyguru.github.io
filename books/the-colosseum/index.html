Inside the Book

The Colosseum opened in Rome in 80 AD, begun by Emperor Vespasian and finished by his son Titus on land where Nero had kept a private lake. Known at the time as the Flavian Amphitheater, it held somewhere between 50,000 and 80,000 people and hosted gladiator fights, animal hunts and public executions for centuries.

This book goes from the engineering, like the numbered gates, the giant sun sails and the elevators that hauled animals up through the floor, to the strange afterlife of the ruins as a fortress, a quarry and a botanist's paradise. It also separates real Roman history from the Hollywood version, including the famous thumbs down.

5 Facts From the Book

FACT #18

Deadly Souvenirs and Gladiator Sweat

Gladiators were Rome's biggest celebrities, and their popularity created a genuinely strange beauty industry. After a match, attendants scraped the mixture of sweat, oil, and dirt off a champion's skin using a curved metal tool called a strigil. That scraped-off residue, known as gloios, was bottled and sold outside the gates to wealthy Roman women, who used it as a facial treatment believed to transfer the fighter's strength and vitality directly onto their own skin. Owning a piece of your favorite gladiator, quite literally, was a real Roman luxury purchase.

FACT #1

The Lake That Became a Stadium

The Colosseum rose on top of an artificial lake that once belonged to Emperor Nero. After the Great Fire of 64 AD, Nero had seized huge tracts of central Rome to build his sprawling Golden House palace complex, complete with its own private lake. When the Flavian dynasty took power after Nero's death, Vespasian saw an opportunity too good to pass up. He drained the tyrant's pleasure lake and built a public amphitheater directly on top of it, turning stolen land back into something the entire city could use. It was propaganda disguised as construction, and it worked spectacularly.

FACT #12

Gladiators Were Mostly Vegetarian Athletes

Forget the beefy Hollywood warrior. Chemical analysis of gladiator bones found at Ephesus shows a diet built mostly around barley, oats, and boiled beans, a cheap, high-carbohydrate regimen that packed on a layer of subcutaneous fat. That fat was not an accident of poor nutrition. It cushioned blood vessels and organs from shallow sword cuts, producing dramatic, bloody-looking wounds that thrilled a crowd without actually killing the fighter. To recover after brutal training, gladiators reportedly drank a bitter tonic made from charred plant ash dissolved in water, an ancient version of an electrolyte drink.

FACT #35

A Botanist Catalogued an Accidental Jungle

By the 19th century, the abandoned, overgrown Colosseum had become a genuine paradise for botanists. Its high stone walls trapped moisture and blocked wind, creating a unique microclimate inside the ruins. In 1855, English botanist Richard Deakin published an entire book cataloguing 420 distinct plant species growing there. Some of these species were unusually rare for the region, and Deakin himself speculated their seeds may have arrived on the coats or in the droppings of exotic animals imported centuries earlier for the games, though wind and birds likely played a role too.

FACT #87

The Ancient Graffiti Left by Bored Fans

Wander the Colosseum's upper corridors today and you can still spot ancient graffiti carved directly into the stone by spectators sitting through long, slow matches. These carvings include crude caricatures of famous gladiators, tallies of betting scores, short poems, and romantic declarations. Some bored fans even carved complete game boards, including three-in-a-row and backgammon-style grids, directly into the marble seats, letting them pass the slower midday execution intermissions with a quiet dice game while waiting for the afternoon's main event to finally begin.

Frequently Asked Questions About The Colosseum

What is the Colosseum?

The Colosseum is an ancient Roman amphitheater, the largest ever built, located in Rome, Italy. It's an iconic symbol of imperial Rome.

Where is the Colosseum located?

The Colosseum is located in the city of Rome, Italy, just east of the Roman Forum. It's easily accessible in the historic center.

Why was the Colosseum built?

The Colosseum was built by Emperor Vespasian and completed by Titus to host gladiatorial contests, animal hunts, and public spectacles for the Roman people.

How old is the Colosseum?

The Colosseum was completed in 80 AD, making it over 1,940 years old. Construction began around 70-72 AD.

What was the original name of the Colosseum?

The original name was the Flavian Amphitheater, named after the Flavian dynasty of emperors who built it. "Colosseum" came later.

Can visitors enter the Colosseum today?

Yes, visitors can enter the Colosseum today. It is a major tourist attraction, offering tours of its various levels, including the arena floor and underground Hypogeum.

How many spectators could the Colosseum hold?

The Colosseum could hold an estimated 50,000 to 80,000 spectators. Its efficient design allowed for quick entry and exit for massive crowds.

Why is part of the Colosseum ruined?

Part of the Colosseum is ruined due to earthquakes, stone quarrying for other buildings during the Middle Ages, and general neglect over centuries.

What happened in the Colosseum?

Gladiatorial contests, animal hunts (venationes), public executions, re-enactments of famous battles, and dramas were held in the Colosseum for centuries.

Can you see the underground area of the Colosseum?

Yes, you can see the underground area, known as the Hypogeum. This complex network of tunnels and chambers housed gladiators, animals, and stage machinery.
