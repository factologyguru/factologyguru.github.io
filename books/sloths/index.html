Inside the Book

Sloths are tree-dwelling mammals of Central and South America that live on a diet of leaves most animals ignore. They run their bodies at a fraction of the usual energy budget, and that strategy has carried them through millions of years. They belong to the same group as armadillos and anteaters, and their extinct relatives included ground sloths the size of elephants.

This book covers how sloths digest their food, why algae and moths live in their fur, how they hang, swim and survive falls, and which myths about them are wrong. It also looks at the threats they face today, from cold snaps to power lines to the tourist selfie trade.

5 Facts From the Book

FACT #5

The Energy Budget of a Lightbulb

Sloths have the lowest metabolic rate of any non-hibernating mammal, operating on a caloric budget that would make a nutritionist weep. Their metabolic rate is only about 40% to 45% of what you would expect for an animal of their size. They have very little muscle mass and a low body temperature that fluctuates with the environment, similar to a reptile. By keeping their internal "engine" on the lowest possible setting, they can survive on a diet of nutrient-poor leaves that would leave any other mammal weak with hunger. They are the biological equivalent of a car that gets 500 miles to the gallon but maxes out at five miles per hour.

FACT #41

The Myth of the 20-Hour Nap

For a long time, it was believed that sloths slept for 20 hours a day. This "fact" came from studies of sloths in captivity, who were bored and had nothing to do. However, recent studies using wireless brain-monitoring devices on wild sloths in Panama showed that they actually sleep about 9 to 10 hours a day, roughly the same as a tired human. They spend the rest of their time moving very slowly, feeding, or just hanging out. They aren't as lazy as we thought; they just do everything with a level of deliberation that we mistake for sleep.

FACT #12

The Moths That Farm Algae

The sloth moth (Cryptoses choloepi) is a tiny insect that lives its entire adult life in the fur of a sloth. These moths don't just live there; they are part of a complex farming system. When the sloth descends to the ground to use the bathroom, the female moths hop off to lay their eggs in the fresh dung. Once the larvae hatch and mature, they fly up into the canopy to find a new sloth host. The moths die and decompose in the fur, providing nitrogen that fertilizes the growth of the algae the sloth eats. It is a perfectly closed loop of nutrients moving between an insect and a mammal.

FACT #7

The Giant Ancestors of the Past

The modern sloth is a pint-sized version of its prehistoric ancestors, which were some of the most formidable mammals on the planet. Megatherium, the giant ground sloth, was the size of a modern elephant and weighed up to four tons. These massive beasts didn't climb trees; they walked the plains of South America and could stand on their hind legs to reach the tops of ancient forests. They survived until about 10,000 years ago, meaning early humans likely hunted these slow-moving mountains of meat. The small sloths we see today are the survivors of a lineage that once dominated the Americas.

FACT #35

The Genetic Link to Armadillos

Sloths belong to the superorder Xenarthra, a group of mammals that also includes armadillos and anteaters. These animals all share unique skeletal features, particularly in their joints, which give them their name (meaning "strange joints"). While an armadillo in a shell and a sloth in a tree look nothing alike, they share a common ancestor that lived millions of years ago. They all have relatively low metabolic rates and specialized diets, proving that the "slow and steady" approach to life is a family tradition that has worked for a very long time.

Frequently Asked Questions About Sloths

Why are sloths so slow?

Sloths are slow because they have an extremely low metabolic rate, about half of what would be expected for their size. This is an adaptation to their low-energy diet of leaves, allowing them to conserve energy efficiently.

How do sloths sleep without falling?

Sloths sleep without falling due to specialized muscle arrangements and strong claws that act like hooks. Their powerful grip allows them to hang upside down effortlessly, even while completely relaxed and asleep in the trees.

Can sloths swim?

Yes, sloths can swim very well and are surprisingly good at it. They can hold their breath for a long time and move three times faster in water than on land, using a dog-paddle stroke.

What do sloths eat?

Sloths primarily eat leaves, buds, and twigs, especially from trees like Cecropia. Their diet is low in nutrients and difficult to digest, contributing to their slow metabolism and lifestyle.

Where do sloths live?

Sloths live in the tropical rainforests of Central and South America. They are arboreal, spending nearly their entire lives hanging upside down from branches in the forest canopy.

Why do sloths have algae on their fur?

Sloths have algae on their fur because their slow movement and damp environment create a perfect habitat for it. This symbiotic relationship provides camouflage, helping them blend into the green canopy.

How many species of sloths are there?

There are six living species of sloths, divided into two main groups: two-toed sloths (Choloepus) and three-toed sloths (Bradypus). Each group has distinct characteristics and geographic distributions.

Can sloths be fast when needed?

No, sloths cannot be fast when needed. Their physiology is optimized for slowness and energy conservation. While they can move slightly quicker in water, they remain inherently slow creatures on land.

What is a sloth's lifespan?

A sloth's lifespan varies by species and environment. In the wild, they typically live between 20 to 30 years, though some individuals in captivity have lived into their late 30s.

Where do sloths spend most of their time?

Sloths spend most of their time in the trees, specifically in the canopy layer of tropical rainforests. They eat, sleep, mate, and give birth while hanging upside down from branches.
