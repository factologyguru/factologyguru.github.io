FACT #1

The Scissor-Marked Specimen of 1799

When the first platypus skin arrived in England, Dr. George Shaw of the British Museum was so certain it was a "manufactured" chimera, similar to the fake "mermaids" sold by sailors, that he actually attempted to cut the beak off to find the stitches. To this day, the original specimen preserved in the Natural History Museum in London still bears the physical scars of Shaw’s skepticism, serving as a permanent testament to the fact that the platypus is so strange it literally broke the brains of the world’s leading scientists.

FACT #7

Biofluorescence: The Ghostly Green Glow

In a discovery that rocked the zoological world in 2020, scientists found that platypus fur is biofluorescent, meaning it absorbs UV light and re-emits it as a ghostly bluish-green glow. This trait, shared with some fungi and deep-sea fish but very few mammals, suggests that platypuses might use this glow to see or communicate with each other in the dim light of dawn and dusk, though the exact evolutionary purpose remains a hotly debated mystery.

FACT #3

The Invisible Milk Sweat Secret

While the platypus is a mammal and feeds its young milk, it possesses no nipples, a fact that baffled researchers for decades until they realized the mother literally sweats milk. Specialized mammary glands under the skin secrete the nutrient-rich fluid onto a "milk patch" on her abdomen, where the young puggles must lap it up like thirsty sponges as it pools in the grooves of her skin and fur.

FACT #41

The Venom That Defies Morphine

If you are ever unlucky enough to be struck by a male platypus’s spur, don't expect a trip to the ER to be easy. Doctors have reported that the pain from platypus venom is largely unaffected by morphine or other standard opioids. The venom contains a cocktail of proteins, including Defensin-like proteins (DLPs), that directly attack the nerve receptors responsible for pain signaling. The result is a "whole-body" pain response that can cause the victim’s lymph nodes to swell and their muscles to atrophy, with sensitivity remaining for months after the initial strike.

FACT #8

The 10-Chromosome Genetic Puzzle

While humans have a simple XX or XY sex-determination system, the platypus carries a staggering ten sex chromosomes, arranged in a complex chain of XXXXX or XYXYXYXYXY. What is truly mind-blowing is that these chromosomes don't look like those of other mammals; they share significant genetic sequences with the ZW chromosome system found in birds, suggesting the platypus is a genomic "missing link" between the world of scales and the world of fur.

Frequently Asked Questions About Platypuses

What is a platypus?

A platypus is a semi-aquatic egg-laying mammal (monotreme) native to eastern Australia, known for its duck-bill, beaver-like tail, and otter-like body.

Where do platypuses live?

Platypuses live in freshwater systems, such as rivers, lakes, and streams, across eastern Australia, including Tasmania. They prefer areas with vegetated banks for burrowing.

Why are platypuses unique?

Platypuses are unique because they are one of only five living monotreme species, meaning they are mammals that lay eggs instead of giving birth to live young. They also have electroreception.

How do platypuses hunt for food?

Platypuses hunt underwater by closing their eyes, ears, and nostrils, using electroreceptors in their bill to detect the electrical impulses of prey like insect larvae and crustaceans.

Can platypuses be dangerous?

Yes, male platypuses can be dangerous. They possess venomous spurs on their hind ankles, which can deliver a painful, though rarely fatal, sting to humans.

What do platypuses eat?

Platypuses primarily eat aquatic invertebrates, including insect larvae, freshwater shrimp, worms, and yabbies. They forage along the bottom of rivers and streams.

How long can a platypus hold its breath underwater?

A platypus can typically hold its breath underwater for about 1 to 2 minutes while foraging, though they can stay submerged for up to 10 minutes if necessary.

Why do platypuses have a bill like a duck?

Platypuses have a bill like a duck to help them forage. It's a highly sensitive sensory organ, equipped with electroreceptors and mechanoreceptors to detect prey underwater.

Can platypuses see underwater?

No, platypuses cannot see underwater. They close their eyes, ears, and nostrils when submerged, relying entirely on their bill's electroreceptors and touch to navigate and find food.

What is the conservation status of platypuses?

The conservation status of platypuses is "Near Threatened." Their populations are declining due to habitat loss, water pollution, climate change, and predation.
