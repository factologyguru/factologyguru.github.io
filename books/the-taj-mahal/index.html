Inside the Book

The Taj Mahal is a marble tomb that was built to look perfect from every angle, yet it hides forced perspective, foundations sunk into the Yamuna riverbed and a tomb that breaks its own symmetry. This book covers the monument itself, from its inlaid gemstones and its gardens to the engineering that has kept it standing for almost four centuries.

It also tells the human story, from Mumtaz Mahal and Shah Jahan to the palace coup that locked the emperor inside Agra Fort. Along the way it separates the legends from the facts, including the black twin that was never built.

5 Facts From the Book

FACT #4

An Emperor Trapped by Walls

A few years after the tomb was finished, Shah Jahan's son Aurangzeb staged a violent takeover, eliminated his rivals, and locked his own father inside Agra Fort instead of killing him outright. For the final eight years of his life, the emperor lived under house arrest in a marble pavilion with one small mercy built into the punishment: a window facing straight across the river at his wife's tomb. He spent nearly a decade staring at the monument he built, unable to visit it, guarded by the son who took everything else from him. Grief built the Taj Mahal. A different kind of grief spent eight years watching it from a cage.

FACT #5

Disguised as a Construction Mess to Fool Bombers

During World War Two, British officials in India worried the Taj Mahal's gleaming white dome would work as a navigation beacon for enemy bombers heading toward Agra. Their solution was refreshingly low-tech. Workers built a massive scaffold of bamboo poles around the dome, turning the world's most recognizable silhouette into what looked, from thousands of feet up, like a chaotic pile of construction debris or overgrown jungle scrub. The building survived the war untouched. Decades later, during the 1965 and 1971 conflicts with Pakistan, authorities repeated the trick with dark canvas sheeting instead of bamboo, proving that sometimes the best defense against modern warfare is just making yourself look boring.

FACT #9

The Black Twin That Was Never Built

A seventeenth-century French traveler named Jean-Baptiste Tavernier popularized a legend that Shah Jahan planned a mirror-image black marble mausoleum on the opposite riverbank for himself, linked by a silver bridge. Archaeologists did find dark stones across the river in the Mehtab Bagh gardens, which fueled the story for centuries. Recent excavation revealed those stones were simply white marble that had oxidized black after generations of exposure to river water and industrial pollution. There was never a black Taj. Shah Jahan always intended to rest beside his wife, not across the river from her, which somewhat undercuts the romance of the legend while confirming a quieter one instead.

FACT #13

The Wife Who Was More Than a Grieving Footnote

Arjumand Banu Begum, given the title Mumtaz Mahal, was far more than a passive royal wife. Court records describe her as Shah Jahan's closest confidante, someone he consulted on matters of state and brought along on military campaigns rather than leaving behind in comfort. Some accounts credit her with holding significant influence over the imperial seal used to finalize decrees. Her death matched the scale of her life. In 1631, while traveling with her husband during a military campaign near Burhanpur, she died from complications delivering their fourteenth child. The emperor lost his closest advisor and companion in one single, brutal night, and built an empire's fortune into stone because of it.

FACT #21

Perfect Symmetry with One Glaring Exception

The entire Taj complex is obsessed with mirror-image balance. Draw a line down the center from north to south and nearly every element on one side matches the other exactly, down to fountains, windows, and inscriptions. There is exactly one break in that discipline: Shah Jahan's own tomb. Because he was buried beside his wife after his death rather than planned into the original symmetrical layout, his larger cenotaph sits noticeably off-center, throwing off the geometry of the entire chamber. The most mathematically perfect building of its era has one flaw at its exact heart, and that flaw is the emperor himself.

Frequently Asked Questions About The Taj Mahal

What is the Taj Mahal?

It is an iconic ivory-white marble mausoleum in Agra, India, commissioned by Emperor Shah Jahan to house the tomb of his favorite wife, Mumtaz Mahal. It is a UNESCO World Heritage site.

Why was the Taj Mahal built?

It was built by Mughal Emperor Shah Jahan as a mausoleum for his beloved wife, Mumtaz Mahal, who died during childbirth. It serves as a symbol of eternal love and devotion.

Where is the Taj Mahal located?

It is located on the right bank of the Yamuna River in the city of Agra, Uttar Pradesh, India.

How long did it take to build the Taj Mahal?

It took approximately 22 years to complete, with construction beginning around 1631 AD and finishing by 1653 AD, employing thousands of artisans and craftsmen.

What architectural style does the Taj Mahal represent?

It represents a magnificent blend of Persian, Islamic, and Indian architectural styles. Its most striking features include the large white marble dome and four minarets.

Can visitors go inside the Taj Mahal?

Yes, visitors can enter the complex and view the cenotaphs of Mumtaz Mahal and Shah Jahan, located in the main mausoleum chamber. Photography is restricted inside the tomb.

Who designed the Taj Mahal?

While the chief architect is often credited as Ustad Ahmed Lahori, it was a collaborative effort involving a council of architects and artisans under Emperor Shah Jahan's patronage and vision.

What materials were used to build the Taj Mahal?

The primary material is white Makrana marble from Rajasthan, adorned with intricate inlays of semi-precious stones like jade, crystal, lapis lazuli, amethyst, and turquoise.

How is the Taj Mahal preserved today?

It is maintained by the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI). Strict regulations are in place to protect its marble from pollution and environmental damage, including limits on nearby vehicles and foot traffic.

Why is the Taj Mahal considered one of the New7Wonders of the World?

Its unparalleled architectural beauty, historical significance, and the enduring love story it represents have earned it global recognition as a masterpiece of human artistry and emotion.
