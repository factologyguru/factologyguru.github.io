Inside the Book

Ching Shih was born around 1775 into a poor boat family in Guangdong and worked the floating brothels of the Pearl River before marrying a pirate captain in 1801. When he died in 1807, she took over his fractured coalition and turned it into the largest pirate confederation on record.

This book follows how she did it and is honest about where the legend runs ahead of the records, from the size of her fleet to the terms of her marriage. What is not in doubt is remarkable enough: she fought imperial fleets and a Portuguese navy, forced the Qing Emperor to bargain with her, and in 1810 walked away with both her freedom and her fortune.

5 Facts From the Book

FACT #4

Negotiating Her Own Terms

Popular accounts describe her refusing to simply accept Zheng Yi's proposal, instead negotiating her own position within the marriage before agreeing to it. No primary Chinese source confirms the often repeated fifty percent figure, so the precise terms remain tradition rather than documented fact. What can be said with confidence is that she entered this partnership as a genuine stakeholder rather than an afterthought, at a moment when most women in her circumstances would have quietly accepted whatever protection was offered. That single act of negotiation, real or embellished in the retelling, set the tone for every decision she made afterward.

FACT #9

Protection for Female Captives

One striking element of her fleet's code addressed the treatment of captured women directly. Rape of a female captive was punishable by death under her rules, and captured women could reportedly marry into the fleet under some form of consent rather than becoming pure property. This stood in sharp contrast to the reputation pirates carried across most of the world during this era. Whether every captain under her enforced this consistently is impossible to confirm fully today. The code's mere existence within her confederation marks a genuine departure from typical piracy of the period, and it is well documented.

FACT #12

A Strategic New Partnership

Shortly after Zheng Yi's death, Ching Shih formed a partnership with Cheung Po, the adopted son who led one of the confederation's most powerful divisions, and the two were reportedly married within weeks of her widowhood. Whatever the personal dimension of this relationship, it carried undeniable strategic value, binding the strongest remaining commander directly to her leadership at the exact moment she needed it most. It kept men who might otherwise have questioned a woman's authority focused instead on the familiar face standing publicly beside her, buying her the time to consolidate real control behind the scenes.

FACT #14

An Emperor Who Could Not Win

Emperor Jiaqing treated Ching Shih's confederation as more than a criminal nuisance, seeing it as a direct challenge to imperial authority on the water. Multiple naval campaigns launched specifically against her fleet failed to break it, and her confederation reportedly captured and repurposed imperial vessels along the way. Losing repeatedly to a coalition led in large part by a woman with no formal rank was a humiliation the imperial court could not spin into any kind of victory. Jiaqing's failure to defeat her by force is precisely what eventually pushed his government toward negotiation instead of continued fighting.

FACT #15

Beaten by the Portuguese

Between September 1809 and January 1810, a Portuguese flotilla stationed in Macau engaged Ching Shih's confederation repeatedly at what became known as the Battle of the Tiger's Mouth, and the fighting did not favor her fleet. Superior gunnery under commander José Pinto Alcoforado e Sousa gave the Portuguese the edge in several clashes, costing her ships and men she could not easily replace. These defeats weakened her position considerably and helped push her toward negotiating amnesty in early 1810, rather than proving her dominance over a European colonial power the way earlier legend has sometimes claimed.

Frequently Asked Questions About Ching Shih

What was Ching Shih famous for?

Ching Shih was famous for being one of the most successful female pirates in history, commanding the vast Red Flag Fleet in the South China Sea.

How did Ching Shih rise to power?

She inherited command of the Red Flag Fleet after her pirate husband, Zheng Yi, died. She solidified her authority through strategic alliances and strict discipline.

Where did Ching Shih operate her pirate fleet?

Ching Shih primarily operated in the South China Sea, terrorizing coastal towns and trade routes around Guangdong province in China.

Can you describe the size of Ching Shih's fleet?

Yes, at its peak, her Red Flag Fleet reportedly comprised over 1,800 ships and more than 80,000 pirates, making it one of the largest pirate confederations ever.

Why was Ching Shih's pirate code so effective?

Her strict code, which included rules against rape and unauthorized desertion, maintained discipline and loyalty among her vast crew, contributing to her fleet's success and cohesion.

What happened to Ching Shih after her pirating career?

Ching Shih accepted an amnesty offer from the Qing government. She retired from piracy and reportedly ran a successful gambling house and salt smuggling operation.

How did Ching Shih manage to defeat imperial forces?

She employed superior tactics, including blockades and ambushes, and leveraged her fleet's size and discipline, often outmaneuvering the Qing dynasty and Portuguese navies.

Who was Ching Shih's husband?

Ching Shih's husband was Zheng Yi, a prominent pirate leader who adopted her and later married her. She inherited his fleet upon his death.

Why is Ching Shih considered such a significant figure in pirate history?

She is significant because she commanded an enormous pirate confederation, successfully challenged imperial powers, and retired peacefully, unlike most pirate captains.

What was Ching Shih's birth name?

Ching Shih's birth name was Shi Xianggu. The name 'Ching Shih' means 'Zheng's wife' (Zheng Yi Sao) in Cantonese, referring to her husband Zheng Yi.
