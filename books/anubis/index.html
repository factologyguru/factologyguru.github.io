Inside the Book

Anubis was the jackal-headed Egyptian god of mummification and the afterlife. He guided souls through the underworld and supervised the weighing of the heart against a feather in the judgment hall. In some of the oldest surviving texts he predates Osiris.

This book covers his origins, the priests who wore his mask, the embalming rituals, the monster waiting under the scales, and the vast dog catacombs at Saqqara. It also follows his worship into the Greek and Roman worlds and shows how far the real Anubis is from the movie villain.

5 Facts From the Book

FACT #1

The Jet Black Desert Scavenger

Ancient Egyptians did not give Anubis a jackal head because it looked fierce. They noticed real jackals digging up shallow graves at the desert's edge, circling cemeteries like uninvited guests. So they flipped the threat into a bodyguard, turning the ultimate grave robber into the ultimate grave protector. The color choice was just as deliberate. Real jackals are sandy brown, not black, but black was the color of the Nile's fertile silt after the flood, the mud that grew Egypt's food. A jackal painted the color of good soil was not a symbol of death. It was a promise of rebirth wearing sharp teeth.

FACT #12

The Monster Waiting Under the Scale

Anubis did not run the judgment hall alone. Crouched beneath the golden scales sat Ammit, a creature stitched together from a crocodile's head, a leopard's front half, and a hippopotamus' rear end, three of the most feared animals in the Egyptian landscape rolled into one. If a heart failed the test against the feather, Ammit devoured it on the spot. This was not symbolic punishment. Losing your heart to Ammit meant total annihilation, no afterlife, no second chance. Egyptians did not fear a fiery hell. They feared simply ceasing to exist, chewed up by a monster built from nightmares.

FACT #19

Millions of Mummies, Some of Them Empty

The Anubeion and similar sites produced staggering numbers of animal mummies, dogs especially, purchased by pilgrims as votive offerings. Modern CT scans of these bundles have revealed something unexpected: a notable portion contain incomplete remains, bones from multiple animals, or in some cases no animal at all, just linen, mud, or sticks shaped to resemble a mummified creature. Rather than pure fraud, researchers suggest demand for millions of votives simply outpaced supply, pushing embalmers to stretch materials however they could. Even the god of honest judgment could not stop his own worshippers from occasionally cutting corners on the production line.

FACT #27

The Underground Dog Catacombs

Beneath the sands of Saqqara lies one of Egypt's most staggering archaeological finds: a network of tunnels containing the mummified remains of an estimated eight million dogs. Pilgrims from across the empire brought animals here as offerings, and excavators found the remains packed floor to ceiling throughout the galleries. The sheer scale dwarfs almost every other animal cult site in Egypt, suggesting devotion to Anubis reached deep into ordinary daily life rather than staying confined to royal temples. Most Egyptians could never afford a lavish human burial, but they could scrape together the cost of a small dog mummy.

FACT #20

The Greek God Fusion Dance

When Alexander the Great's conquest brought Greek culture into Egypt, the two religious systems did not fight over which death-guide deity was correct. They merged them. Greeks recognized Anubis' role as guide of souls and matched it to Hermes, their own messenger god who ferried spirits to the underworld. The result was Hermanubis, a hybrid deity with a Greek athletic body and Anubis' jackal head, carrying a caduceus and drawing worship across the Mediterranean for centuries. It is one of history's cleanest examples of religious efficiency: rather than debate theology, two civilizations just spliced their gods together.

Frequently Asked Questions About Anubis

What was Anubis's primary role in ancient Egyptian mythology?

Anubis's primary role was the god of mummification and the afterlife, guiding souls and overseeing the weighing of the heart ceremony.

Why is Anubis often depicted with the head of a jackal or dog?

Anubis is depicted with a jackal or dog head because these animals were scavengers often seen near cemeteries in ancient Egypt, associating them with death and the underworld.

How was Anubis involved in the mummification process?

Anubis was believed to be the patron god of embalmers. Priests performing mummification often wore Anubis masks, symbolically embodying him during the ritual to prepare the deceased for the afterlife.

Where was Anubis worshipped most prominently?

Anubis was worshipped throughout Egypt, but his cult center was in Cynopolis (modern El Qais), also known as 'City of the Dog,' located in the 17th Upper Egyptian nome.

What was Anubis's relationship with Osiris?

Anubis played a crucial role in Osiris's resurrection. He embalmed Osiris's body, making him the first mummy, and protected his corpse, thus becoming associated with funerary rites.

Can Anubis be considered a benevolent or malevolent deity?

Anubis is generally considered a benevolent deity. While associated with death, his role was to guide and protect the dead, ensuring their safe passage to the afterlife, not to harm them.

How did Anubis's role evolve over time in Egyptian mythology?

Anubis's role evolved, initially being the chief god of the dead before Osiris's cult gained prominence. He then became Osiris's assistant, focusing on mummification and guiding souls.

What symbols are associated with Anubis?

Symbols associated with Anubis include the jackal, the flail, and the ankh. He is often shown holding a staff or an ankh, representing life.

Why was the weighing of the heart ceremony important to Anubis?

The weighing of the heart ceremony was crucial because Anubis oversaw it, ensuring the deceased's heart was balanced against Ma'at's feather. This determined if they were worthy of the afterlife.

Can Anubis be found in the Book of the Dead?

Yes, Anubis features prominently in the Book of the Dead. He is depicted guiding the deceased, performing rituals, and supervising the judgment of the soul in the Hall of Two Truths.
